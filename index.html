Question + model answer
1. Explain politics as state power and as compromise and consensus. Discuss four strengths and four weaknesses of each definition.

Politics has several meanings because it involves power, public decisions and ways of managing disagreement. Two useful definitions are politics as state power and politics as compromise and consensus.

Politics as state power is the struggle to acquire, control and use authority through the institutions of the state. In Ghana, political parties compete in elections to form a government; the winners then make and implement decisions under the Constitution. State power can maintain law and order because public institutions can make rules and enforce them. It provides leadership by giving officials authority to coordinate national decisions. It can support development when governments direct public resources to schools, health services and infrastructure. It also enables national defence and public safety through accountable security institutions.

However, state power can be abused when leaders use public authority for private benefit. If power is concentrated and checks are weak, it can encourage authoritarian rule and restrict rights. Competition to capture office may intensify polarisation or violence, especially when political rules are not trusted. Control of public appointments and contracts can also create opportunities for corruption and favouritism. These risks show why constitutional limits, independent scrutiny and peaceful transfers of power matter.

Politics as compromise and consensus is the process of negotiating among people with different interests so they can reach a decision. A compromise occurs when each side adjusts some demands; consensus means broad agreement, though not necessarily unanimity. Parliamentary debate, consultation with affected communities and negotiated policy changes can put this approach into practice. It promotes peaceful coexistence by giving groups a way to manage disputes without violence. It encourages cooperation toward shared goals. It strengthens participation when different groups can voice their views. Negotiation can also reduce conflict by helping parties accept a workable settlement.

This approach has limitations too. Negotiations take time and may delay urgent action. A compromise can leave all sides dissatisfied if each gives up important priorities. Consensus is difficult when interests or values are deeply opposed. Unequal bargaining power can let influential groups shape negotiations and pressure weaker groups into an unfair agreement. For that reason, compromise needs transparent procedures, rights protections and fair representation.

In conclusion, state power explains who holds public authority and how it can be used, while compromise and consensus explain how people with competing interests can reach decisions. Political life requires both effective institutions and fair ways to negotiate their use.

2. Is the study of politics scientific? Explain using Aristotle and Gabriel Almond.

Political science studies political institutions, behaviour, power and public decisions. It uses evidence and systematic methods, but it cannot predict human behaviour with the precision expected in some natural sciences. Aristotle and Gabriel Almond help explain both the case for treating politics as a science and the limits of that claim.

Aristotle studied constitutions and compared forms of government, asking who ruled and whose interests government served. His work supports the scientific view because he classified political systems, observed how institutions operated and used comparison to reason about their strengths and failures. He also treated political life as a subject that could be investigated systematically. At the same time, his normative question about the good life shows that political inquiry includes value judgements as well as observable facts. His ancient polis and restricted account of citizenship also limit direct application to modern democratic states.

Gabriel Almond developed comparative and behavioural approaches to political science. He argued that political systems and their institutions can be studied by examining their structures, functions and behaviour across cases. This approach encourages researchers to define concepts, gather evidence, compare systems and test explanations. It helps move analysis beyond personal opinion and makes political claims open to review. Its limits include the difficulty of measuring concepts such as legitimacy, the risk that categories built for comparison may not fit every society, and the influence of researchers’ assumptions. Political behaviour can also change in response to events, so findings rarely produce exact predictions.

Political science has several scientific features: it asks focused questions, collects evidence through methods such as interviews and surveys, compares cases, develops explanations and revises conclusions when new evidence appears. Yet political research cannot usually control all the conditions that shape an election or policy outcome. People interpret events differently, institutions vary, and values such as justice or equality are contested. These features make predictions uncertain and require care when applying a finding to another place or time.

In conclusion, politics can be studied scientifically through systematic observation, evidence and comparison. Aristotle’s constitutional analysis and Almond’s comparative study of political systems illustrate that approach. Political science is best understood as an empirical social science, but not an exact science: its subject is human beings acting in changing social and political contexts.

Learning outcomes & reading plan

By the end of these units, students should be able to define key political concepts; compare theories and institutions; explain core political processes; apply concepts to Ghana; and construct evidence-based essay answers.

  1. Nature and scope of political science
  2. Nation, state and nation-state
  3. Power, authority, influence, legitimacy and sovereignty
  4. Political ideology
  5. Government and systems of government
  6. Democracy and good governance
  7. Political socialisation, political culture and participation
  8. Political processes and research methods

Core course material: your POLI111 Combined Slides, Ruth POLI lecture slides, The ABC of Political Science by Isaac Brako and Kwame Asah-Asante, and the Marcus POLI111 past questions.

Companion reading: introductory texts on political institutions and constitutions deepen units 2, 3, 5 and 6. At the University of Ghana, Political Institutions is listed as POLI 112, a separate companion course; this guide keeps its assessments within the eight POLI 111 topics.
